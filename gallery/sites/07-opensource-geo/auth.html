TrustPlane Auth · the runtime

The proof-bound authorization runtime.

TrustPlane Auth is a drop-in verifying boundary that runs in your infrastructure, right in the request path. It checks a request-bound, single-use proof on every call, decides locally without phoning home, and forwards only what it can actually prove.

Request-bound proofs, not standing tokens

Each call carries a proof tied to its parameters. Tamper the path or replay the proof and verification fails closed, so a compromised or injected agent can't act beyond the call it was authorized for.

# sign one specific call
$ trustplane sign --method GET --path /orders \
    --audience acme.orders
✓ allow   verified · single-use · audited

# replay the same proof?
✗ deny    jti_replay
# tamper the path after signing?
✗ deny    request_binding_mismatch

A drop-in boundary, no rewrite required

Inside the runtime

What TrustPlane Auth enforces.

Signature & identity verification
Validates the caller's identity and the proof signature against trusted anchors.
Replay protection
Single-use proof consumption rejects replayed credentials outright.
Request binding
Method, path, body and time are bound into the proof; any mismatch denies.
Policy & freshness
Enforces signed policy with freshness guarantees so stale policy never silently applies.
Multi-anchor sources
Accept callers from multiple trust anchors with per-route source rules.
Local verification
Decisions are made at the boundary, with no per-request call back to a central service.
Evidence-grade audit
Each allow/deny is recorded and tied to the exact request it bound.
Stable deny reasons
Clear, machine-readable reasons (e.g. jti_replay) for every denial.
Open standards

Built on identity, not in place of it.

TrustPlane doesn't reinvent identity, and it doesn't want to replace your IdP. It consumes SPIFFE/SPIRE and OIDC/JWT, and applies the request-bound, proof-of-possession model that the IETF is standardizing through DPoP (RFC 9449), the WIMSE working group, and OAuth Transaction Tokens, pointed at the place most of us have been ignoring: agents and machine systems.
