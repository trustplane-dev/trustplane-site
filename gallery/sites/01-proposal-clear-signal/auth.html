TrustPlane Auth is a free-to-run authorization runtime for machine and AI-agent traffic. It issues and verifies TrustPlane Passports — short-lived, proof-bound authorization artifacts derived from workload identity — and applies local policy before a request reaches the protected service. Verification is offline-capable: issuer keys resolve from signed local trust bundles.

Why request-bound

A key says who you are. A passport says what this request may do.

Short-lived by design

Passports expire in seconds to minutes, not quarters. A leaked passport is a shrinking problem; a leaked API key is a standing one.

Bound to intent

The proof covers the request being made — route, method, audience — so authority can't quietly stretch to actions nobody approved.

Replay is a first-class enemy

JTI tracking rejects the second use of a passport. A recorded request can't be replayed into a second refund.

Deployment

Three ways in. None of them a rewrite.

Auth meets your services where they are — including the ones nobody wants to touch.

The adapter, for brownfield services.A verifier-forwarding proxy that sits in front of an existing HTTP service. Requests that fail verification never reach the service; no application changes required.
Middleware, for Go services.Drop-in HTTP middleware and reverse-proxy primitives when you'd rather verify in-process.
Gateway and sidecar patterns.Run verification at the API edge, beside a workload, or in front of MCP and tool servers — Kubernetes-compatible, not Kubernetes-dependent.
Trust material

Decisions from signed files, not from someone's uptime.

Verifiers accept a request when issuer, trust domain, subject, key binding, and context match local policy. That policy — and the keys behind it — arrives as signed bundle documents you can inspect, pin, and mirror.

Offline verification.

Bundle documents give verifiers issuer public keys without a mandatory online lookup. Your authorization boundary works during an internet outage — and during ours.

Pluggable identity.

TrustPlane-native issuers, OIDC/JWT issuers, and SPIFFE/SPIRE integrations share the same passport semantics. Bring the workload identity you already have.

Fail closed, explain why.

Unknown route, missing trust material, unsupported proof profile — the verifier denies and says exactly why. Deny reasons are for debugging, not divination.

FAQ

Auth, in five honest answers.

Is Auth really free? What's the catch?

It's free to run, with docs and community support. The catch is the honest kind: when your fleet grows, you'll want enrollment, signed policy rollout, revocation, and audit from one place — that's Control, and that's what we charge for.

What identity systems does it work with?

OIDC/JWT issuers, SPIFFE/SPIRE, and TrustPlane-native issuers. Auth consumes workload identity; it doesn't replace it.

What does “proof-bound” mean concretely?

The passport carries a confirmation claim binding it to the presenting client and intended request context. Verification checks that binding along with signature, issuer, audience, expiry, trust domain, and replay state — seven checks per decision.

How does policy get to the verifier?

On Develop, you place signed trust-material and policy bundles as files. With Control, the same artifacts are versioned, signed, and distributed to your Auth Sites — and revocation propagates the same way.

What languages are supported?

The runtime and middleware are Go; the adapter protects services written in anything, because it works at the HTTP boundary.

Adapter in front, passports required, deny reasons you can read. Start where the risk is and grow from there.
